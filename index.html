Doctoral research interest

My proposed work sits between two literatures that rarely speak to each other.

The first is the process tradition in identity development — Luyckx's five-dimensional model and Crocetti's extension of it, which treat identity not as a fixed outcome but as repeating cycles of exploration and commitment. The second is the dimensional turn in personality pathology, particularly Criterion A of the DSM-5 Alternative Model, where identity appears as a component of personality functioning itself.

The question I want to answer is whether the association between identity processes and features of personality pathology holds in culturally plural, non-Western samples — and whether individualism–collectivism moderates it. Nearly all instrument development in this area has happened in Western settings, which makes cross-cultural measurement invariance a live methodological problem rather than a footnote.

Research

Café Spaces as Emotional Anchors

  • MSc dissertation, UEL
  • 2025
  • Supervisor: Dr Sonya Dineva
  • 15 interviews · 7 café / 8 home
  • Under review
Question
Why do people choose to work in cafés rather than at home, and what does that choice do to their motivation and emotional regulation?
Method
Qualitative and exploratory. Semi-structured interviews with 15 adults in Mumbai — 7 who regularly work or study in cafés, 8 who work from home — analysed with reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke), using a hybrid inductive–deductive approach.
Finding
Three themes: ambience, culture and emotional regulation. Cafés function as “third places” combining the comfort of home with the stimulation of a public setting. Sensory ambience and the visible presence of others working produced novelty, flow and a form of informal accountability, while repeated contact with staff and regulars created belonging. Home offered autonomy and control but brought monotony, isolation and blurred boundaries. Some participants were overstimulated by cafés, suggesting individual differences moderate how the same environment is experienced.

Gratitude and Self-Esteem in Young Adults

  • Independent research, NMIMS
  • 2024
  • Supervisor: Dr Heena Kamble
  • n = 98
  • Unpublished
Question
Is dispositional gratitude related to self-esteem, and how do people actually practise gratitude day to day?
Method
Cross-sectional survey of 98 adults aged 18 and over, recruited by convenience sampling. Gratitude Questionnaire-6 (GQ-6) and the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, analysed with Pearson correlation.
Finding
A strong positive correlation between gratitude and self-esteem, r = .78, 95% CI [0.69, 0.85]. Around 44% of respondents reported practising gratitude daily, yet almost half named no specific method for doing so — suggesting a construct that is widely endorsed and loosely operationalised.

Hesitation Towards Therapy in India

  • Research methods, NMIMS
  • 2022
  • Supervisor: Dr Aditi Dey Sarkar
  • n = 114 + 3 practitioners
  • In preparation
Question
What makes people hesitate to seek psychological help, and how does that hesitation sit inside families rather than in individuals alone?
Method
Mixed methods. Survey of 114 respondents aged 15–80 across Mumbai, Ahmedabad and Cambay, alongside structured interviews with three practising psychologists and psychiatrists.
Finding
Over 70% of respondents said they needed to work on their mental health and two thirds had discussed it with a family member, yet around half still described themselves as hesitant to raise it at home. Fear of being labelled, cost, religious belief and the anticipated reaction of relatives recurred across both strands. This was the study that first pointed me towards collectivist cultural values as an explanatory variable rather than background.
